World Cup Survey Wave 1 · Fielded 5–10 June 2026

The World Cup is here, and we've compared how the trades and the public will watch and engage.

We put the same World Cup questions to two UK crowds: the general public, and verified tradespeople from Voice of the Trade. Much of the time they agree. It's where they part ways that gets interesting, and that's what the eight reads below are about.

1The contradiction, in full

More committed. Less thrilled.

Here's the tension running through everything that follows. The trades watch more of the tournament than the public on every measure that counts, yet feel less excited about it on every measure of mood. Turning up in numbers and being fired up aren't the same thing, and it pays not to confuse the two.

8So what

Three calls for the client brief

None of these gaps is just a curiosity. Each one points somewhere practical, whether that's a different place to spend, a different kind of offer, or a different way to judge whether any of it is working, depending on who a sponsor is really trying to reach.

Budget · 01

Lead with the offer, not the story

60% vs 44%

Discounts and prize draws land far harder with the trades than any purpose-led message, and telling them a brand backs grassroots football barely moves the needle. So for trade-facing work, lead with the actual offer and let the brand story sit behind it.

Channel · 02

Sponsor the room, not the feed

48% vs 25%

The trades engage in person, in pubs, on site and around a workplace sweepstake, while their appetite for following and sharing brands online runs the other way. Money aimed at this audience does more work at the trade counter and the watch-party than it ever will in the feed.

Interpretation · 03

Don't mistake calm for cold

66% vs 47%

The trades sound less excited on every question about mood, yet show up for more of the tournament than the public do. A tracker that leads on excitement will quietly under-rate them, so when you size the trade opportunity, trust what they'll do over how loudly they say they feel.

7Said versus did

Intent ran well ahead of action

Here's the reckoning, and it's the most useful thing in this wave. In June, 60% of trades told us a discount would sway them and only 22% said nothing would shift them. In the event just 20% actually bought or moved, and 58% did nothing at all. The stated levers weren't wrong about what works, but they were badly wrong about how many. Take any Wave 1 intent number and expect roughly a third of it to convert.

9So what

Three calls for the next tournament

Wave 1 told us what these two crowds intended. Wave 2 tells us what that intent was actually worth, and the honest answer is: less than it looked, but not in the places you'd guess. Three things to carry into the next brief.

Planning · 01

Discount intent by three

60% → 20%

Six in ten trades said a discount would sway them; two in ten actually moved. The lever was real, the volume wasn't. Stated-intent questions are fine for ranking what works, but price them at roughly a third when you size the prize, or you'll build a business case the tournament can't pay back.

Channel · 02

Buy the counter, not the bar

29% vs 17%

Wave 1 said sponsor the room. The late kick-offs emptied it: only 17% of trades made it to a pub against the 29% who planned to, while 92% watched at home. The channel that did deliver was in-store, reaching the trade at double the public's rate and the only one that grew. Same instinct, different room.

Audience · 03

The public moved, the trade noticed

32% vs 20%

Flip Wave 1 on its head. The trades noticed more brands and stayed up later, yet the public were the ones who actually bought, 32% against 20%. If a sponsor wants volume this tournament, that's the public. If they want the trade, attention was never the problem: converting it was, and it converts at the merchant counter or not at all.
